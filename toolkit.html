The toolkitFor decisions

The value audit

Three questions to put to any decision, purchase or job offer before you commit. They come from Versed in Value: one question for each kind of value. Work through them in order and answer honestly; the result only reflects what you tell it.

One line, e.g. "Whether to accept the job offer" or "Whether to buy the car".
Economic value

1. Economic value

What is this worth over its whole life, not just today?

  • Add up the full cost: money, time and upkeep, for as long as you'll have it.
  • Name what you give up by choosing it. That is part of the price.

Is it worth more to you than it costs?

Professional value

2. Professional value

What problem does it solve, and for whom?

  • Say the problem in one sentence. If you can't, there may not be one.
  • Name who benefits. Would they agree it's a problem worth solving?

Does it solve a real problem?

Ethical value

3. Ethical value

What does choosing it say about what I stand for?

  • Picture explaining this choice to someone whose opinion you respect.
  • Look for a short-term gain that could cost you trust later.

Does it fit what you stand for?

Answer all three questions to see your result.

The toolkitFor your CV and profile

Say it louder

Most CVs describe what you were responsible for. Readers decide on what changed because of you. This tool helps you say that, without saying more than is true.

Climb the ladder

Every line about your work sits on one of four rungs. Aim for rung 3 or 4 on the lines your reader sees first.

The value ladder: four rungs from duty to worth
RungWhat it saysExample
1. DutyWhat you were responsible forResponsible for month-end reporting
2. ActionWhat you actually didRebuilt the month-end reporting pack
3. ResultWhat changedRebuilt the reporting pack, cutting the month-end close from eight days to five
4. WorthWhy it mattered, and to whom…so managers had their numbers before the next month began

The pattern: action + what + result + so that, or for whom.

Build a line

Take one line from your CV or profile and answer three questions about it. The page drafts the sentence and tells you which rung it has reached.

The honesty test, before it goes public

No numbers? Try a lens

Most people say "but I don't have numbers". You don't need them. Ask yourself these eight questions, grouped by the three kinds of value from Versed in Value.

Eight value lenses
LensKind of valueQuestion to ask yourself
MoneyEconomicWhat did it save, earn or recover?
TimeEconomicWhat got faster, or took fewer hours?
ScaleEconomicHow many people, items, pounds or places did it cover?
ProblemProfessionalWhat was broken or missing before, and works now?
FirstProfessionalWhat had never been done before, by you or your team?
QualityProfessionalWhat got more accurate, or had fewer errors or complaints?
RiskEthicalWhat didn't go wrong because you were there?
TrustEthicalWho relies on you now, or came back for more?

When you can't share the figures

If your figures are confidential, don't publish them. Use one of these instead.

Safe swaps for confidential figures
Instead ofUseExample
An exact figureA proportion"Cut processing costs by a third"
An exact figureRelative terms"The fastest close in the team's records"
A client's nameA description"For a national retailer"
Any figureThe decision it enabled"Gave the board what it needed to approve the project"

A CV goes to a few people; a profile goes to everyone. Check your employer's policy before publishing any figure. If in doubt, use the public-safe version everywhere.

The honesty test

Louder, never bigger than the truth. Before a line goes public, ask:

  • Could I talk about this for five minutes in an interview?
  • Would my manager recognise it?
  • Was it "I" or "we"? If "we", say so: "co-led", "as one of a team of five".

Your headline

The same idea works for the line under your name: your job plus your specialism, in the words your reader would search for, plus the thing few people with your title can say.
